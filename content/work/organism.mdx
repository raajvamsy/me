## The question

Most reinforcement learning work tunes an agent to a task. I wanted to test something stricter: one creature that lives continuously, has no hand-written behaviour, and learns from its own experience to stay viable in whatever body it is given.

The claim only counts if it beats its own controls: acting at random, never learning, and stopping learning halfway through life. Protocols and pass/fail gates are frozen before each run, and results are not tuned afterwards. If a mechanism fails its gate, it is reverted and the failure is recorded.

## How the core works

![The core: senses flow into a world model of five small networks, imagination and episodic memory feed a chooser, and habit produces action.](/img/architecture.webp)

The core has no imports or vocabulary from any body, and a test enforces that boundary. It sees only numbered sensor channels and its own needs.

- **World model.** An ensemble of five small networks predicts what happens next, with calibrated uncertainty.
- **Imagination.** It compares candidate plans by simulating them in its model, side by side.
- **Credibility.** When its networks disagree, it trusts its imagination less.
- **Episodic memory.** A fast system recalls what actually happened after similar past choices, and that evidence is combined with imagination by precision.
- **Habit.** Good choices are distilled into habit, but only when real episodes on independent halves of memory confirm them.

![When the ensemble agrees it trusts the prediction; when it disagrees it acts with care.](/img/credibility.webp)

## Results

Each gate is a paired comparison across seeds, reported as a t statistic and the number of lives that passed. These are the frozen final verification at core `ca419e4`.

| Gate | A: arena | B: day-cycle device | C: cyclic forager |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predictions improve | No (2 of 5) | Yes (4 of 5) | Yes (4 of 5) |
| Learned beats newborn | No, t 0.73 (6 of 15) | Yes, t 11.44 (15 of 15) | Yes, t 1.79 (11 of 15) |
| Less time out of range than random and no learning | No, t −1.76 and −1.08 | Yes, t 17.7 and 15.5 | No, t 1.52 and 1.52 |
| Keeps adapting after the world changes | Not applicable | No, t −0.71 | No, t 1.46 |

**The goal is not achieved yet.** The claim holds for Body B and partly for Body C, where it eats credibly more than both controls and keeps energy in range 44% of the time against about 4% for random. It does not hold for Body A, where it explores and eats about as well as random but never learns to steer.

## Learning to speak by ear

Given a body with a throat, a mouth and an ear, the same core learned a recorded vowel perfectly in 3 of 3 lives, with no teacher. On a full sentence it got close and then collapsed. You can [hear both](/research/organism/speaking).

## What failed, and what each failure taught

| Attempt | What broke | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Treating needs as never shared | No-superstition tests | A blanket rule brings superstition back |
| Recency replay for all learning | Held-out value and calibration | Slow learners must interleave old and new |
| Long returns, surprise-prioritised replay | Held-out value tests | Prioritising rare events ruins a general learner's accuracy |
| Episodic memory, attempts 1–4 | Fixture power, context-blind lookup, estimator mismatch | The lookup needs value-relevant features, and must estimate what imagination estimates |
| Distillation, attempts 1–3 | No-superstition test | Winner's curse, and the optimizer's curse on shared model error |
| Curious tie-breaking | Forced-choice test | It changed history before the test |
| Surprise-gated recency | Re-adaptation gate (t −0.32 in B, 1.46 in C) | Removed under its frozen failure rule |
| Curiosity from learning progress, two designs | Did not hold up | Reverted |

![A creature in a room of random food drops refuses to credit its own dance for the food.](/img/superstition.webp)

One test I am most glad I built checks for superstition: the creature must not treat a lucky coincidence as a skill. Several promising mechanisms failed here first.

## The open problem

![A fast episodic learner has too little evidence, a slow world model is general but slow, and forcing either one breaks generalisation.](/img/tension.webp)

Turning rare successes into a multi-step skill is where it stalls. The slow learner is general, but pushing it to credit rare outcomes damages its accuracy. The fast learner is safe, but it has too little evidence: 1 to 7 credible lessons per life in the arena. This is the classic tension in complementary learning systems, and I don't have an answer yet.

## What's next

The current work removes the last scripted schedules, so that when to sleep and replay becomes a learned choice. After that come plans that depend on the observation, which steering seems to need.
