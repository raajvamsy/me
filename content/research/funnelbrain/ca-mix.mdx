## The question

The brain can't speak in fluent sentences. Could it instead grow images by learning a small genome for a cellular automaton?

## The negative result

Sweeping genome size against automaton steps on the target letter "A", with 600 trials each:

| Genome bytes | 0 steps | 1 step | 2 steps | 4 steps | 6 steps |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 16 | 0.531 | 0.766 | 0.750 | 0.703 | 0.688 |
| 32 | 0.656 | 0.750 | 0.719 | 0.688 | 0.719 |
| 64 | 0.781 | 0.750 | 0.719 | 0.703 | 0.656 |
| 96 | 0.812 | 0.734 | 0.734 | 0.734 | 0.734 |

With a large enough genome, zero steps won, which is simply painting pixels directly. The growth story didn't hold as first imagined. Two earlier claims in the notes were also corrected: raw motor bytes don't work as a genome, and the first grid embedding was worse than the original.

## What worked

| Step | Result |
|---|---|
| Multi-state automata | Lifted the ceiling |
| Patch grid, layout and colour searched together | Worked, but slowly |
| Two stages: grey layout, then a direct colour lookup | Flower photo scored 0.974 against a 0.380 baseline, about 12× faster |
| Decomposing complex shapes into patches | A star became recognisable where one canvas gave a blob |

Block seams remain visible, and a brightness-keyed colour table can't separate regions of equal brightness with different hues. Recombining objects, recolouring and grafting regions all work.
